Atlas of the Rieske oxygenase family

A Rieske oxygenase (RO) alpha subunit carries two metal centres: a Rieske [2Fe-2S] cluster that receives electrons and a mononuclear Fe(II) site where the substrate is oxidised. This atlas describes every protein in the database that demonstrably has both, together with the genes around it, the partners that feed it electrons, the regulator upstream of its operon, and the organisms that carry it.

Three units, three different claims. A type is one of the 76 enzymes characterised in the literature and curated here; its substrate is experimental fact. A member is a protein assigned to the type whose profile it scores highest against; this states closest characterised relative, not identical function. A variant is a sequence-homogeneous subgroup of members, the finest unit at which a distinct function can reasonably be proposed. Pages that show substrates always show the evidence level alongside, because in this family references with different substrates reach 99 % identity to each other.

Sections

Phylogeny

Maximum-likelihood tree of the curated references and one representative per variant, built from the aligned catalytic core. Shows which types are lineages and which are arbitrary cuts through a continuum.

Sequence space

Similarity network and type-versus-type identity matrix, with the identities between characterised references as the calibration for any threshold.

Taxonomy

Phylum composition of every type, including the plant, fungal and animal branches that pass the same catalytic-centre test as the bacterial enzymes.

Operons and electron transport

Beta subunits, ferredoxins and reductases detected from neighbour protein sequences rather than annotation text, per type and per RO group.

Regulation

The 5' end of each operon, the intergenic region where its promoter must lie, and the regulator family facing it.

Co-occurrence

Which enzyme types share a genome or an operon more often than chance allows, separating gene duplication from pathways that feed the same intermediate.

Ecology and mobility

Plasmid and transposon association against substrate class, and taxonomic spread as a proxy for how long a type has been inherited vertically.

Novel candidates

Confirmed oxygenases that no characterised enzyme explains, grouped by variant, with the disagreement between the two independent measures of novelty.

Evidence

How close each member actually is to a characterised enzyme, and which types consist almost entirely of distant relatives.

Data quality

Sequence redundancy and its effect on every count, the limits that cannot be measured away, and the script behind every downloadable file.

Statistics

Each association tested twice, once per entry and once per genus, with effect sizes and an explicit verdict on robustness.

What the data support

  • Substrate labels transfer to a minority of members. 24 % of members are at least 60 % identical to a characterised enzyme; in 17 of the larger types (20 members or more) at least 80 % of members are distant or novel. Evidence
  • The beta subunit is a property of the RO group, not of the substrate (Cramér's V = 0.73). Groups 2 to 4 are α₃β₃ enzymes; groups 1 and 5 are almost exclusively α₃. Operons
  • Xenobiotic-degrading types are plasmid-borne far more often than natural-substrate types, 3.2-fold after collapsing repeated strains to one observation per genus. Ecology
  • Plasmid-borne alpha subunits carry more complete operons (1.00 verified partners versus 0.61), as expected for self-contained catabolic modules that move between hosts. Operons
  • Regulators sit head-to-head with the operon, with LysR the dominant family. The regulator is closer to the operon start than a non-regulator gene would be, which is the expected geometry for a shared promoter region. Regulation
  • One alignment column separates the quaternary-amine branch from the rest of the family. The two carboxylates of this fold behave in opposite ways, and only one of them can be read from an alignment column at all. The catalytic carboxylate is a first-shell ligand of the mononuclear iron, and in the structural reference set it is aspartate in 26 of 26 structures — but no single column holds it: it lands within one column of 431 in 20 of them, and the remaining 6 are spread over 407-411 and 449-451. The residue column 431 calls sits 10.9–14.7 Å from the iron in the 20 structures where that residue could be located at all; in 6 more the column call has no counterpart to measure, and 1 structure carries no mononuclear iron. The range is therefore a measurement on 20 structures, not on 26. Either way that column's Asp-to-Glu ratio is not a measurement of the ligand and is not published here. The structural figures in this item come from a one-off audit of the reference structures that the pipeline cannot reproduce: no rebuild step produces it. The column ranges are corroborated by a second file, but the counts cannot be re-derived, so read them as a snapshot of 2026-10-28 rather than as a current measurement. The inter-subunit bridging carboxylate (column 286) does pass the same structural test, and it splits 14,558 Asp to 6,483 Glu, and the glutamate is not scattered: it marks the group-5 enzymes that cleave carbon–nitrogen bonds or demethylate open-chain quaternary amines, measured as 100 % in GbcA (n=505), 92 % in CntA (n=2,562), 89 % in Stc2 (n=1,768), 87 % in BmoA (n=1,160). The per-member residues are still column reads; what the structures verified is that this column reads the bridge. Evidence · the figure and full counts
