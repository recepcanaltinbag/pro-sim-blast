What controls these operons, and what moves them

Three questions about the genes around the oxygenase: which regulator sits with which chemistry, how far the regulator diverges when the enzyme does — measured against the other genes of the same operon, because the pairs are selected on the enzyme and that alone makes everything else look more divergent — and whether the enzymes that move between genomes are really the ones that degrade man-made compounds.

2 · Regulators are the most variable protein in the operon, but much of the gap was how the pairs were selected

Why this section cannot be read without a control gene. The pairs below are drawn from within one enzyme type, and an enzyme type is the profile model its alpha subunit scored best against. High alpha-to-alpha identity inside a type is therefore guaranteed by construction, not observed: it is the selection, not a result. Any other protein in the operon will look more divergent than the alpha subunit for that reason alone, whether or not it evolves faster, so a gap against the alpha subunit is not by itself a finding about the regulator. The only question the data can answer is whether the regulator is more divergent than the other genes of the same operon are. The beta subunit, the ferredoxin and the reductase are therefore scored on exactly the same genome pairs, from the same evidence class as the regulator itself, and none of them took part in defining the enzyme type.

2.3 · What each orthology gate costs, and what it changes

A shared regulator family is not a shared protein. TetR and LysR are very large families, so two TetR-family regulators in two genomes may be entirely different proteins, and a low identity between them measures replacement rather than divergence. Compounding that, the regulator is identified purely by position — the first gene upstream of the operon 5′ end — so a gene that merely happens to sit there can enter the comparison. The ladder below applies successively stricter evidence that the two regulators really are the same protein.

{% if div.floor %}

The third gate needs a floor, and the floor was measured rather than assumed. Scoring {{ div.floor.n_pairs|fmt }} regulator pairs drawn from different enzyme types and different families — pairs known not to be the same protein — gives a mean identity of {{ '%.1f %%' % div.floor.mean }} and a {{ div.floor.floor_percentile }}th percentile of {{ '%.1f %%' % div.floor.measured_floor }}. That is how similar two unrelated regulators can look by alignment alone, so a pair at or below it carries no information about divergence. The earlier version of this analysis took {{ '%.0f %%' % div.floor.assumed_floor_in_the_earlier_version }} from the literature instead, which was too generous.

4 · Mobility, and how much of it is an annotation artefact

The database has reported that enzymes acting on man-made substrates are more often mobile. That claim rested on a variable defined as "on a plasmid or with a transposon nearby", and the transposon half is a regular expression over the GenBank product text, which measures how a genome was annotated. Of {{ rel.totals.get('confirmed', 0)|fmt }} confirmed entries, {{ mob.regex_only|fmt }} are counted mobile only because of that text match. So the claim was recomputed three ways.
