Where the close and the distant relatives were found

If an enzyme type spread from one setting into others, its close and its distant members should have been isolated from different places. This page tests that, type by type, and reaches a mostly negative answer worth understanding. It then asks a sharper version of the same question that does not use the curated reference set at all: if a pollutant-degrading enzyme has a relative in the sea, how far apart are the two, and therefore how recently could they have split?

1 · The pooled picture

Across the whole database, close and distant members do occupy different habitats, but the effect is small. Close members are over-represented in human clinical, gut, contaminated and wastewater settings; distant ones in plant tissue, marine and thermal settings. Habitat is known for only part of the data, so every share below is a share of what is classified, not of everything.

5 · What the reference-based test cannot answer

Evidence levels are a ladder of identity to a curated enzyme, not a phylogeny. A distant relative is not demonstrably older, so "where did this enzyme evolve" is not derivable from these data. What is derivable is whether a type's ecological signature changes with distance from its reference, and for all but the pooled case the answer is that it cannot be separated from taxonomy.
