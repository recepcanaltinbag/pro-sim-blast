Predicted Rieske oxygenases in archaea and fungi

No archaeal or fungal Rieske oxygenase has ever been characterised experimentally. This page is what sequence analysis finds anyway: 109 archaeal and 654 fungal proteins that carry the complete catalytic apparatus of the family. Not one of them has been purified, assayed, or shown to turn over a substrate. They are predictions, and the page is built so that they cannot be mistaken for anything else.

Nothing on this page is part of the curated set. Nothing here enters types/, chemistry.csv, the HMM library, or any statistic in this database. No prediction is assigned an ro_group, none seeds a profile, and none is counted in any figure, ratio or q-value elsewhere in this database. A prediction moves into the curated set only when experimental evidence is published, and then it passes the ordinary admission gates rather than living here.

1 · Why the test can be believed

The claim rests on one thing: a protein is called a Rieske oxygenase only if all four Rieske ligands and the mononuclear 2-His-1-carboxylate triad sit at the model's derived columns. The discriminating power of that rule was measured in the same run, on controls, and the negative controls are the important half.

Fifty-six of the sixty-six true non-Rieske-oxygenases carry all four Rieske ligands, none carries the catalytic triad, and none passes. That is exactly the biochemical distinction the test is meant to draw: false positives 0/66, recovery 75/75.

Low coverage is not a negative. Low coverage is not a negative. Where the alignment never reaches the catalytic columns the result is 'not measurable', not 'no triad'. Every row reports its coverage beside its motif score. This was not a precaution in principle; it was measured. This was measured, not assumed: a 717-residue protein (AerC) aligned over 18.9 % of the model, stopped at column 231, never reached the catalytic columns, and scored 0/3 -- which was wrong. Aligned in fragments, its ligands were all in place. Every row in the downloadable tables therefore carries its coverage beside its motif score, and the two are meant to be read together.

2 · Archaea

109 of 128 archaeal Rieske-domain proteins pass the strict criterion, at 331–505 residues (median 381). Every one of them carries the bridging aspartate. Every one of them is also labelled Predicted (61), Inferred from homology (48), experimental (0) — that is, there is no experimental evidence anywhere in this set.

Not a recent transfer from bacteria

Is there a near-identical bacterial relative, as a recent transfer would leave? The 109 sequences were compared against 22,531 bacterial Rieske-domain proteins (UniProt). The nearest bacterium is 33.4 % identical at the median and 51.7 % at most; 0 exceed 70 % and 0 exceed 90 %. A recent transfer would have left near-identical bacterial copies, and there are none.

But not one lineage either

This was the result that did not go as expected. The tree holds the 109 archaeal sequences, the curated references and 281 bacterial Rieske oxygenases. The archaeal proteins do not form a single clade: they split into 7 pure clusters, each well supported within itself, and each with a bacterial sister group. Halobacteria alone shows at least two independent acquisitions.

3 · Fungi

654 of 2351 pass, at median 26.9 % identity to the nearest curated reference (maximum 38.5 %, none above 50 %).

The fungal candidates sit overwhelmingly nearest to group 5 (633 of 654): CntA 493, CmoB 88, CmoS 50. Sixteen sit nearest to the sterol 7-desaturases Nvd and DAF-36.

The fungal annotations reading 'choline monooxygenase, chloroplastic' are an artefact of transfer from plants. This test reads alignment columns rather than annotations, so the finding does not depend on them. But note that the archaeal set is also mostly nearest to group 5, so the earlier claim that the two sets fall in different places -- and that this argued against a shared artefact -- does not hold.

4 · What may and may not be said

Supported

  • Archaeal proteins with the complete catalytic apparatus exist and are not few: 109, across three archaeal classes, at 23-44 % identity to the nearest curated bacterial reference over 84-98 % of their length.
  • They are not a recent acquisition: no bacterial relative exceeds 51.7 % identity in a set of 22,531.
  • They are not one lineage: seven pure clusters, each strongly supported within itself, each with a bacterial sister group.
  • Halobacteria alone shows at least two independent acquisitions.

Not supported

  • That any of them has Rieske oxygenase activity. None is characterised; all are labelled Predicted or Inferred from homology.
  • Any particular donor group. Deep node support is 0.52-0.97, so the backbone is poorly resolved.
  • The direction of transfer. The tree was rooted at its midpoint.
  • Bootstrap or AU support. FastTree SH-like local support was used instead.
Two numbers on this page were reported wrongly first and have been corrected.
  • The length range was reported as 429-505 aa. It is 331-505 aa, median 381.
  • The nearest references were reported as 'all group 3 (PhdA, NarAa, NidA)'. Group 5 is in fact the majority (61 of 109, CntA alone 59); group 3 is second with 44.

Cause. The first run read only the top forty rows sorted by coverage, and the top five DIAMOND hits per phylum, then generalised. The highest-identity hits really were PhdA, NarAa and NidA; the bulk was CntA.

What this costs the argument. The argument that archaea and fungi fall in different places, and that this is evidence against a shared artefact, is void. Both sets are mostly nearest to group 5. A shared artefact is not excluded by this measurement and needs its own control.

Still standing: the controls (0/66 false positives, 75/75 recovery); the HGT test (maximum 51.7 %); the phylogeny: seven pure clusters, not monophyletic; the bridging aspartate in 109 of 109.
