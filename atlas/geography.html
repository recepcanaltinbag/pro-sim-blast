Where these enzymes have been seen

8,875 of 21,046 confirmed alpha subunits (42 %) come from a record that states where the sample was collected, across 121 distinct places. That is enough to draw a map. Whether the map means anything is a separate question, and it is the one this page is organised around.

This is in large part a map of sequencing capacity. A country here is where a genome was collected and deposited, not where the enzyme lives. The United States alone carries 23 % of the entries that have a country at all, which says more about who runs sequencing centres than about Rieske oxygenases. Three numbers are therefore given for every place and they answer different questions: raw entries (most biased), distinct genera (strain duplicates removed), and the deviation of each enzyme type from the overall distribution — the last being the only one from which anything is inferred, because sampling effort appears on both sides of that comparison and largely cancels.
What counts as a genus here, and what is left out

A genus is read from the NCBI taxonomy rank of the genome record that carries the entry: the nearest ancestor whose rank is exactly genus. It is not taken from the first word of the organism name. That difference is measured, not cosmetic: the first word merges every Candidatus organism into one bucket that actually holds 17 separate genera, it carries over quoting and bracketing from the source string ('Nostoc, [Enterobacter]), and it misses renamings — 360 confirmed entries change genus when the rank is used (Sinorhizobium → Ensifer, Rhizobium rhizogenes → Martinezella).

What is left out of every genus count on this page. 867 confirmed entries (4.1%) have no genus-rank ancestor at all in the current NCBI taxonomy. The largest case is Mycolicibacterium, which the organism strings still name and which would be this database's seventh largest “genus”: NCBI now holds it as a subgenus, Mycobacterium (subgen. Mycolicibacterium), and that lineage runs from species straight to the family. Those entries are neither dropped nor given an invented genus; they are carried with the state unassigned and the nearest named rank beside them, and they are excluded from the genus counts shown here. The taxonomy page gives the full measurement.

Does any enzyme type come from a different set of places?

Comparing one type's country distribution against the country distribution of all confirmed entries removes most of the sampling bias, because the same bias is in both. The test is the one used throughout the statistics page: a standardised residual per cell, Benjamini–Hochberg corrected, reported only when the difference is also at least ten percentage points, and repeated after collapsing every type, genus and country to a single observation.

Of 333 type-by-country cells tested, 9 differ materially from the overall distribution and 5 survive the genus-level check. That is a small number, and it is the honest answer: once sequencing effort is divided out, almost no Rieske oxygenase type is geographically distinctive.

  • PTC52 is over-represented in China: 43 % of its located entries against 10 % overall (+33 points), and it holds after collapsing type, genus and country to one observation.
  • CAO is over-represented in China: 36 % of its located entries against 10 % overall (+26 points), and it holds after collapsing type, genus and country to one observation.
  • PhnA1a is over-represented in China: 29 % of its located entries against 10 % overall (+19 points), and it holds after collapsing type, genus and country to one observation.
  • PhtAa is over-represented in Cambodia: 20 % of its located entries against 4 % overall (+16 points), and it holds after collapsing type, genus and country to one observation.
  • PDO is over-represented in Australia: 15 % of its located entries against 4 % overall (+10 points), and it holds after collapsing type, genus and country to one observation.
  • PAO is over-represented in China: 32 % of its located entries against 10 % overall (+22 points), but it does not hold once repeated strains are collapsed, so it is most likely one deeply sequenced project.
  • ROCH34 is under-represented in USA: 11 % of its located entries against 23 % overall (-12 points), but it does not hold once repeated strains are collapsed, so it is most likely one deeply sequenced project.
  • CmoS is over-represented in China: 22 % of its located entries against 10 % overall (+12 points), but it does not hold once repeated strains are collapsed, so it is most likely one deeply sequenced project.

Where the main variant sits, and where the others do

Within a type, entries that share an identical sequence variant are one observation of one thing. Comparing where the most common variant was collected against where the remaining variants were collected asks whether a type's diversity sits in one place or is spread out. The same caveat applies throughout: a variant found only in one country may simply be a variant only one country has sequenced.

How this was built

Locations come from the /geo_loc_name or /country qualifier of the source record, stored verbatim and split at the first colon. 121 distinct strings appear; 112 map onto a country code, and the rest are oceans, seas and two dissolved states, listed above rather than discarded. No coordinate was inferred and no record was reassigned. Computed by geography.py. Download geography.json

ROAR-DB, the Rieske Oxygenase Annotation Resource. Built from RefSeq and GenBank annotation with profile models of 76 Rieske oxygenases characterised in the literature. Every figure states its method and its limits; what the database cannot tell you is documented alongside what it can.
