What controls these operons, and what moves them

Three questions about the genes around the oxygenase: which regulator sits with which chemistry, how far the regulator diverges when the enzyme does — measured against the other genes of the same operon, because the pairs are selected on the enzyme and that alone makes everything else look more divergent — and whether the enzymes that move between genomes are really the ones that degrade man-made compounds.

2 · Regulators are the most variable protein in the operon, but much of the gap was how the pairs were selected

Why this section cannot be read without a control gene. The pairs below are drawn from within one enzyme type, and an enzyme type is the profile model its alpha subunit scored best against. High alpha-to-alpha identity inside a type is therefore guaranteed by construction, not observed: it is the selection, not a result. Any other protein in the operon will look more divergent than the alpha subunit for that reason alone, whether or not it evolves faster, so a gap against the alpha subunit is not by itself a finding about the regulator. The only question the data can answer is whether the regulator is more divergent than the other genes of the same operon are. The beta subunit, the ferredoxin and the reductase are therefore scored on exactly the same genome pairs, from the same evidence class as the regulator itself, and none of them took part in defining the enzyme type.

2.1 · Every protein in the operon, measured on the same pairs

Each row is the identity between the two copies of one protein, set against the identity between the two alpha subunits of the same pairs. The gap is positive in every row, which is exactly what selecting the pairs on the alpha subunit produces. Only the differences between the rows carry information.

2.2 · The regulator against each control, pair by pair

This is the comparison the claim stands or falls on. For every pair carrying both the regulator and one control protein at both ends, the two identities are compared directly. The alpha subunit does not enter at all: the question is only which of two operon genes is the more variable.

Stated the way the controls allow: regulators are the most variable protein in the operon, but every operon component is more variable than the alpha subunit, and the alpha subunit's conservation is an artefact of selecting the pairs on it.

2.3 · What each orthology gate costs, and what it changes

A shared regulator family is not a shared protein. TetR and LysR are very large families, so two TetR-family regulators in two genomes may be entirely different proteins, and a low identity between them measures replacement rather than divergence. Compounding that, the regulator is identified purely by position — the first gene upstream of the operon 5′ end — so a gene that merely happens to sit there can enter the comparison. The ladder below applies successively stricter evidence that the two regulators really are the same protein.

The third gate needs a floor, and the floor was measured rather than assumed. Scoring 4,000 regulator pairs drawn from different enzyme types and different families — pairs known not to be the same protein — gives a mean identity of 24.8 % and a 95th percentile of 31.4 %. That is how similar two unrelated regulators can look by alignment alone, so a pair at or below it carries no information about divergence. The earlier version of this analysis took 25 % from the literature instead, which was too generous.

2.4 · The gap against the alpha subunit, band by band

This is what the section used to report on its own. It is kept because the shape is real and section 3 refers to it, but it is a gap against the protein the pairs were selected on, so it measures the regulator and the selection together and cannot be read as a divergence rate by itself. Pairs are restricted to those whose regulators share a family, which is gate 2 above.

3 · The same chemistry under different control

A regulator can be replaced rather than merely diverge. Comparing the regulator family at both ends of a pair answers how often that happens.

At near-identical enzymes the regulator family is always the same; below 60 % identity it differs in about seven pairs out of ten. So as an enzyme type spreads and diverges, the same chemistry repeatedly comes under a different kind of transcriptional control. This is also why the unrestricted comparison overstates regulator divergence, and why section 2 excludes these pairs.

4 · Mobility, and how much of it is an annotation artefact

The database has reported that enzymes acting on man-made substrates are more often mobile. That claim rested on a variable defined as "on a plasmid or with a transposon nearby", and the transposon half is a regular expression over the GenBank product text, which measures how a genome was annotated. Of 0 confirmed entries, 3,018 are counted mobile only because of that text match. So the claim was recomputed three ways.

6 · Is a transposon a property of the species or of the enzyme?

Both, but the enzyme more. Each factor is measured as the share of variance it explains in the binary outcome, next to what the same factor explains after the labels are shuffled. That column is essential here: genus has 508 levels and enzyme type only 61, and a factor with more levels explains more by chance alone.

7 · Do similar enzymes have similar operons?

They do, and the expectation is worth stating because its failures are interesting. Each measure below is shown against the level reached by pairs from different enzyme types, which is the floor: every operon contains the alpha subunit, so any similarity measure starts above zero.

8 · Limits

  • The transposon calls are never verified against sequence. The regular expression also matches integrase, recombinase and resolvase, which are not always transposon components, so the category is an upper bound on text matches and says nothing about what is actually in the DNA.
  • The regulator family label comes from the same kind of /product regular expression, so the first question rests on the same annotation chain as the bias measured in the second. The internal consistency check is the geometry: family labels that reproduce the classical orientation and spacing of their family are more believable than ones that do not.
  • No promoter sequence was examined. 88.9 % of the source GenBank records are CON entries with no sequence in the file, so operator sites and -35/-10 boxes cannot be inspected and the regulator association cannot be confirmed at the level of DNA binding.
  • The plasmid field is GenBank's own declaration. Integrated plasmids and unnamed megaplasmids are missed, so it is an undercount, but it is a property of the molecule rather than a choice of words.
  • Nothing here is causal. Both questions measure co-occurrence in sequenced genomes, and the sampled genomes are not a sample of nature: PAH and xenobiotic degraders are a well studied group, which biases annotation richness and isolate counts in the same direction as the hypotheses.
  • Chemical family is a property of the enzyme type, not of the entry, so the regulator-by-family tables are aggregations over at most 61 types. A family carried by two or three types can be moved by one type.
  • The third question identifies a regulator by POSITION, as the first gene upstream of the operon 5' end. No binding was measured, so 'the regulator of this enzyme' is an inference from gene order. A regulator acting from elsewhere on the replicon, or a global regulator, is invisible to it.
  • The third question selects its pairs on the alpha subunit, because an enzyme type IS the profile the alpha subunit scored best against. High alpha-to-alpha identity inside a type is therefore a property of the selection, not an observation, and any gap measured against it is uninterpretable on its own. That is why the same contrast is computed for the beta subunit, the ferredoxin and the reductase on the same pairs; the regulator claim rests entirely on the margin over those controls and not on the gap itself.
  • Pairwise identity saturates. The floor was measured rather than assumed, from regulator pairs taken from different enzyme types and different families, and below it the measure cannot separate a fast-diverging regulator from an unrelated protein. No divergence rate is claimed in the bands where most pairs sit at that floor.
  • The strictest orthology gate, a reciprocal best hit inside the enzyme type, is conservative in one direction only. A fast-diverging regulator is exactly the one whose best match in its own type is somebody else, so the gate removes the pairs the claim is about: a gap that survives it is real, a gap that disappears there is ambiguous.
