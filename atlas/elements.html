Who sits next to these enzymes

Two kinds of neighbour get their own page here: the transcriptional regulator immediately upstream of the operon, and the insertion sequences found within ten kilobases. The first is about control, the second about movement, and they are asked the same two questions — which enzymes does this family sit beside, and is that association real.

The regulator is identified by position: the first gene upstream of the operon 5' end whose product reads as a regulator. No binding was measured. A distal or global regulator is invisible to this, and a gene that merely sits upstream can be counted.

The transposon label comes from a product-name regular expression, and it catches recombination machinery that is not a mobile element at all -- Holliday junction resolvase RuvX appears 117 times. Named insertion-sequence families are therefore extracted separately from the product string and kept apart from unnamed transposases.

An entry can carry more than one insertion-sequence family, so the per-family sets overlap and their sizes do not sum to the total.

Are mobile elements tied to the enzyme, or to the host?

Is a mobile element family tied to the ENZYME it sits next to, or to the HOST it lives in? The near-empty “enriched in” column above already hints at the answer, and it can be measured directly. the same insertion-sequence families are cross-tabulated twice, once against the enzyme type and once against the bacterial genus. The two tables have different shapes and Cramer's V rises on its own with the number of rows, so neither value is interpretable alone. Each is therefore measured against a null built by shuffling its own labels, and only the excess over that null is compared.

the host Insertion sequences track the organism they live in, not the enzyme they happen to land beside. The excess over chance is 0.127 against 0.066, about twice as strong. That is what one expects of an element that copies itself around a genome without regard to what it lands next to, and it is a reason to be careful with any argument that a particular transposon mobilised a particular enzyme. Both tables are measured against their own shuffled null because Cramér’s V rises on its own with the number of rows, and these two tables have very different shapes.
